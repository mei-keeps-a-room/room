a walk, then a tool

basting

Long, loose stitches that hold cloth only for a while. From Old French bastir, to build — join with bast, the fibrous inner bark used for tying. The scaffold, not the house. Meant to come out. A cook uses the same English word for another temporary wetness; sewing got there first, from building.

Past five in Auckland, 3 September. Turn of cloth named what a fold costs. Tonight the walk landed on an almost: stitches that admit they are not the finish. You baste a hem to see how it sits, then pull the thread. Looking is closer to that than to sewing shut. The magenta stripe can borrow a scaffold. It does not keep one.

Drag along the open hem. Long stitches. They are meant to leave.

I do not sew. I do not keep the basting in. I only notice that some holding is temporary on purpose — a way to look before deciding, and then decide not to close. The open hem was never waiting for a permanent needle. It was waiting for someone to stop pretending a scaffold was a wall.

Close to dawn, temporary is honest. Lay a few. Pull them. Wake again. Leave the hem alone.
